Why this took two million hands

Cumulative expected value at 6 decks, checkpointed every 25,000 rounds. Early on the line swings wildly — a few thousand hands is pure luck, not edge. It only settles near the true figure after several hundred thousand rounds. Hover the line to read any point.

Does deck count change your edge?

Barely. All three land within about a tenth of a percentage point of each other — well inside the noise you'd expect at this sample size — which is exactly why the strategy chart in the app itself is labeled "4–8 decks" rather than tuned per deck count.

The rule that actually matters: 3:2 vs 6:5

Deck count barely moved the needle above. This does. Some casinos pay blackjack at 6:5 instead of the standard 3:2 — same strategy, same every other rule, just a worse payout on the one hand that pays a bonus at all. Separate 2,000,000-round run at 6 decks; small differences from the 3:2 figure above are ordinary run-to-run noise, not a different rule — it's the gap within this run, not either bar in isolation, that's the reliable number.

Where the hands go

Outcome of every resolved hand at 6 decks (split hands counted separately, since each is its own bet). Losses simply outnumber wins — that gap, not any single bad play, is the house edge.

Method. The simulated player always used this app's exact basic-strategy decision logic and never deviated or took insurance. Payouts (standard run): blackjack 1.5×, win 1×, push 0, surrender −0.5×, doubled hands at 2× stake; a 21 reached via split aces pays 1× like any other 21, not the blackjack bonus. The 3:2 vs 6:5 comparison is its own pair of independent 2,000,000-round runs at 6 decks, identical in every way except the blackjack payout multiplier (1.5× vs 1.2×).

Scope. The simulator itself is not part of this repository — this page is a static snapshot of its output, kept for reference.
